# Issue #003 — Community vs. Audience

**Monday, June 30, 2026 · Community**

---

## Opening Thought

There's a creator I follow — tens of thousands of subscribers, consistent views, solid brand deals — who told me recently that she feels completely alone in her work. Posts go up. Numbers go up. Comments come in. And she still feels like she's talking to a wall.

An audience watches you. A community is *with* you.

The distinction matters more than most creator advice acknowledges. An audience can be large and completely passive. It can disappear the moment the algorithm shifts, or the moment a competitor makes something shinier. A community is stickier, harder to build, and worth ten times as much — not just financially, but in terms of how the work actually feels to make.

This doesn't mean chasing engagement metrics. It means orienting toward people, not numbers. It means replying to the reply, not just the comment. It means knowing, by name, the twenty people who care most about what you do — and treating that like the real asset it is.

The uncomfortable truth is that most creator platforms are optimized for audiences, not communities. They're designed to maximize passive consumption, which benefits the platform. Building community on top of those platforms requires a deliberate counter-effort: going slower, going deeper, giving people a reason to talk to each other and not just to you.

Your twenty most engaged followers know more about what you should make next than any analytics dashboard. Have you talked to them lately?

## The Gear Take

This one isn't for streaming — or not only for streaming. The Stream Deck is a programmable macro pad with physical buttons you assign to any action: launch an app, trigger a scene change, send a keyboard shortcut, paste a text snippet, post to social, toggle a mute. 

For community-oriented creators specifically, it's useful for: one-button Zoom go-live, scene switching during recorded calls, quick-launching Discord, and pasting pre-written replies to common questions without losing the conversation flow. It sounds trivial. In practice it cuts the mechanical overhead of live interaction by a meaningful amount, which means you're less in your head and more present.

The software (Elgato Connect) is free and works on Mac and Windows.

## Three Resources

**1. Circle (community platform)**
If you're thinking about moving your audience somewhere you own — away from Instagram or YouTube comments — Circle is the cleanest option right now. It has spaces (like channels), courses, events, and a member directory. Starts at $89/month, which only makes sense once you have paying members. But worth knowing before you need it.

**2. "Get Together" — Bailey Richardson, Kevin Huynh, Kai Elmer Sotto**
A practical, case-study-heavy book on how real communities start and grow. Co-written by former Meetup team members. Less theory, more process. Useful for creators who want a framework for intentional community-building, not just "be authentic."

**3. The "Who Not How" mental shift**
Book by Dan Sullivan, but the core idea can be summarized: when you hit a limit in your creative work, the instinct is to figure out *how* to do the thing yourself. The better question is *who* can help. For community building, this means: who in your existing audience has skills you don't? Who wants to contribute, not just consume? Finding those people is how communities start.

## The Work

**Creator Spotlight: Priya Mehta, @slowstitchclub**

Priya runs an embroidery community that started as a comment section on her tutorials. She noticed the same twenty usernames appearing on every video, sometimes helping each other troubleshoot in the replies.

She moved those twenty people into a group chat. Then a Discord. Then a monthly Zoom call that now regularly has 60+ attendees. She's never run an ad. She hasn't needed to — the community brings new members in on its own.

Her newsletter has 3,400 subscribers. Her Discord has 890 members. By every metric that matters to her livelihood, the 890 are worth more.

*"The algorithm will always have a new algorithm. But people who've made things together don't forget that."*

## One Prompt

**Name your twenty.**

Who are the twenty people who engage most genuinely with your work? If you can't name them, that's information. If you can, pick three and reach out this week — not to sell anything, not to ask for anything. Just to say: *I noticed you, and I appreciate it.*
